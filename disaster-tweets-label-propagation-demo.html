Real Tweets, Real Ambiguity

This version uses actual tweets from Kaggle's "NLP with Disaster Tweets" competition — the same dataset behind your RNN notebook. Every review in the last version was invented to make a point. These are real people's real tweets, and the ambiguity you'll run into is the actual, famous reason this exact classification task is hard.

Source: Kaggle, "Natural Language Processing with Disaster Tweets" (train.csv), 7,613 tweets labeled disaster / not-disaster. 26 tweets were selected here and screened for profanity, slurs, and political content before use. A handful had trailing question-mark artifacts (from emoji lost in CSV export) trimmed for readability; wording is otherwise verbatim.

What just happened

Only four tweets ever had a real, checked label — the same handful you'd realistically have time to read yourself out of 7,613. Every round, each unlabeled tweet looked for its closest labeled neighbor and inherited that label if it was close enough. That's semi-supervised learning working exactly as intended.

But notice what "close enough" actually meant here. Real tweets are short, so word counts land on only a handful of distinct values — several genuinely different tweets score identically. "Forest fire near La Ronge Sask. Canada" and "I forgot to bring chocolate with me. Major disaster." both score one disaster-word, zero casual-words. To this feature representation, they are the same tweet. That's not a limitation of this demo; it's the real, well-documented reason the "NLP with Disaster Tweets" competition is considered a hard problem — sarcasm, idioms, song titles, and drills all use disaster vocabulary without describing a disaster, and simple word-counting has no way to tell the difference.

This is exactly why modern NLP has moved past counting words at all. A large language model reads "I'm setting myself up for disaster" in context and understands it's an idiom about a bad decision, not an emergency — because it's learned from how words like these actually get used across billions of sentences, not just whether specific keywords appear. That's the leap from Unit 2's counting-based methods to Unit 3's generative and contextual models.

Feature collision
