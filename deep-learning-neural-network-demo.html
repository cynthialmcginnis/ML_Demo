Why Deep Learning Needs More Than One Line

The regression demos in this series fit something straight through the data: a line, then a plane. Some real patterns can't be captured that way, no matter how you angle it. This one can't be fixed by adjusting two numbers — it needs a genuinely different kind of model. That's the actual reason "deep" learning exists.

The story: a factory molds rubber seal rings. Each ring is measured on two things during production — how far its curing temperature drifted from target, and how far its molding pressure drifted from target. A ring that's off-target on both by too much, combined, leaks and fails inspection — even if either reading alone looks fine. That "combined distance from target" rule is standard in precision manufacturing (engineers call it a circular tolerance zone), and it's exactly why the true Pass/Fail boundary is a ring, not a line.

  1. Step 1Try to separate Pass from Fail with a single straight line. Watch the accuracy ceiling — no angle or position gets you close to 100%.
  2. Step 2Click "Train the Network" to add a hidden layer of neurons and watch the boundary bend to actually fit the pattern.
  3. Step 3Change how many hidden neurons the network has, and watch what it can and can't learn to represent.

Seal Ring Inspection: Pass or Fail?

Each dot is one seal ring. Position shows how far its curing temperature and molding pressure drifted from target, in standardized deviation units (0 = exactly on target).

The rule being applied: combined drift = √(temp² + pressure²). A ring passes if that combined drift is under about 0.42; it fails if it's over about 0.68. (The gap between those two numbers is deliberate, to keep this a clean teaching example — real tolerance zones don't leave gaps.) Notice the rule never looks at temperature or pressure alone. It's their combination that decides, which is exactly what makes the boundary a ring instead of a line.
8 rings (dashed outline) are deliberately mislabeled — a stand-in for real inspection error. A well-trained network should still get most of these wrong, on purpose. If it ever hits 100%, that's a sign it's memorizing exceptions instead of learning the real rule.

What just happened

A straight line has exactly two things you can adjust: which way it points, and where it sits. That's genuinely all the freedom it has. When the true boundary between Pass and Fail is a ring around a target, no setting of those two numbers does better than roughly splitting the data in half by luck — the shape a line can draw and the shape the data actually needs are fundamentally different things. More tweaking doesn't fix that. A different kind of model does.

A hidden layer of neurons changes what shape is even possible to draw. Each hidden neuron computes a weighted sum of the two inputs plus a bias — the same math your regression models used — but then bends the result through a non-linear function (here, tanh) before passing it on. Pushing an input all the way through every layer like this, until the network produces a prediction, is called the forward pass. One bent line still isn't much. Several bent lines, combined by the output neuron, can wrap around a shape a single line never could. That combination is what "deep" refers to: not one weighted sum, but layers of them, each one allowed to bend.

Training the network runs the same idea as "Reveal Best-Fit" from the earlier demos — search for the numbers that minimize error — just with far more numbers being searched at once. After each forward pass, the prediction gets compared against the known Pass/Fail label for that unit — that comparison is the supervised part; there's no error to correct without a real answer to check against. The error then flows backward through every layer, computing how much each weight contributed to the mistake. That backward pass is backpropagation. The diagram above the Train button shows those numbers directly: every line is one real weight, teal for positive, brick for negative, thickness for magnitude. Watch it while training runs — the lines are moving because the numbers are changing, not for decoration. Try dropping the hidden neuron count to 1 or 2: the network usually can't fully wrap the boundary anymore. There isn't enough flexibility. Push it up to 6 or 8 and it usually can. That trade-off between how much a network can represent and how many parameters it costs is the central design question in deep learning, and it's the same question whether the network has 8 neurons or 8 billion.

Notice accuracy tops out around 93%, no matter how many hidden neurons you give it or how long you train. That's not a limitation of the network — it's the 8 deliberately mislabeled rings (dashed outline) doing their job. They sit solidly in "obviously Pass" or "obviously Fail" territory but carry the wrong label, standing in for real inspection error. A network with enough neurons technically could bend its boundary into a strange shape to loop around and catch those 8 too, but a well-behaved one won't — smoothly curving around real noise costs more error elsewhere than it's worth. That restraint is worth watching for. A network that stretches itself thin to fit every last point, including the outliers, isn't smarter; it's overfitting, and it usually performs worse on new data it hasn't seen.

Hidden layer
A layer of neurons between input and output that the model computes internally, not something you feed in directly.
Forward pass
Pushing an input all the way through the network, layer by layer, until it produces a prediction.
Activation function
The non-linear bend (here, tanh) applied after each weighted sum — without it, stacking layers would still only draw straight lines.
Capacity
Roughly, how complex a shape a network can represent — more neurons, more capacity.
Backpropagation
The method for computing how every weight in every layer should change to reduce error, working backward from the output.
Epoch
One full pass of training over every example in the dataset.
Overfitting
When a model bends itself around noise or outliers instead of the real pattern — often looks like better training accuracy, but performs worse on new data.
